# Number Theory: GCD & LCM

---

## 1. Definition

In elementary number theory and abstract algebra, the **Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)** and the **Least Common Multiple (LCM)** are dual operations that quantify the multiplicative relationship between pairs or sets of integers. They reveal how numbers share common factors and intersect along common multiples.

### Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)

Formally, let $a$ and $b$ be two integers, where at least one is non-zero. The **Greatest Common Divisor**, denoted as $\gcd(a, b)$, is the largest positive integer $d \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$ that satisfies the following two algebraic criteria:

1. **Common Divisor Condition:** The integer $d$ divides both $a$ and $b$ simultaneously:
   $$d \mid a \quad \text{and} \quad d \mid b$$
2. **Maximality Condition:** If there exists any other integer $c$ that is a common divisor of both $a$ and $b$, then $c$ must also divide $d$ (which implies $c \le d$):
   $$\forall c \in \mathbb{Z}, \quad (\text{if } c \mid a \text{ and } c \mid b, \text{ then } c \mid d)$$

### Least Common Multiple (LCM)

Conversely, let $a$ and $b$ be two non-zero integers. The **Least Common Multiple**, denoted as $\text{lcm}(a, b)$, is the smallest positive integer $m \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$ that satisfies the following two algebraic criteria:

1. **Common Multiple Condition:** The integer $m$ is a multiple of both $a$ and $b$ simultaneously:
   $$a \mid m \quad \text{and} \quad b \mid m$$
2. **Minimality Condition:** If there exists any other integer $n$ that is a common multiple of both $a$ and $b$, then $m$ must divide $n$ (which implies $m \le n$):
   $$\forall n \in \mathbb{Z}, \quad (\text{if } a \mid n \text{ and } b \mid n, \text{ then } m \mid n)$$

---

## 2. Key Terminology

To analyze common factors, common multiples, and their applications in modular frameworks, the following vocabulary must be established:

- **Divisor / Factor:** An integer $d$ that divides another integer $a$ completely, leaving no remainder ($a = d \cdot k$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$).
- **Multiple:** An integer $m$ generated by multiplying a given integer $a$ by any whole number ($m = a \cdot k$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$).
- **Coprime / Relatively Prime:** A relationship between two integers $a$ and $b$ where they share no common positive factors other than 1. This is written formally as:
  $$\gcd(a, b) = 1$$
- **Euclidean Algorithm:** An ancient, highly efficient iterative method that calculates the greatest common divisor of two integers by repeatedly computing remainders.
- **Bézout's Coefficients:** The pair of integers $(x, y)$ that satisfy Bézout's Identity for a given equation: $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$.
- **Pairwise Coprime:** A property of a set of integers where every possible pair within the set is completely coprime to one another.
- **Canonical Prime Factorization:** The unique breakdown of an integer into a product of prime numbers raised to positive integer powers.

---

## 3. Purpose

The dual mathematical operations of GCD and LCM act as structural tools for optimizing periodic systems, simplifying fractions, and resolving linear equations over integer boundaries.

### Simplifying Rational Systems and Fractions

When adding, subtracting, or comparing fractions with different denominators, the **LCM** provides the lowest common denominator needed to combine the terms without changing their values. Conversely, the **GCD** identifies the largest common factor shared by a numerator and denominator, allowing fractions to be simplified to their lowest terms.

### Solving Linear Diophantine Equations

In pure algebra, equations whose solutions are restricted strictly to the set of integers are called **Diophantine Equations**. The linear form of these equations is written as:

$$ax + by = c$$

A linear Diophantine equation can be solved if and only if the greatest common divisor of the coefficients $a$ and $b$ perfectly divides the constant term $c$ ($\gcd(a, b) \mid c$). The GCD determines whether an integer solution path exists.

### Real-World Engineering and Computing Applications

- **Scheduling and Synchronizing Periodic Events:** If two automated industrial machines run on different repeating schedules—for example, Machine A resets every 12 minutes and Machine B resets every 18 minutes—the **LCM** determines exactly when both machines will reset at the same time ($\text{lcm}(12, 18) = 36$ minutes).
- **Network Infrastructure Routing:** Internet data routers use GCD calculations to align data packets into uniform sizes, reducing fragmentation across transmission paths.
- **Cryptographic Keys (The RSA Algorithm):** Public key encryption relies on generating keys using functions like Euler's Totient Function ($\phi(n)$) or the Carmichael function ($\lambda(n)$), which are calculated using GCD and LCM operations on large prime numbers.

## 4. Fundamental Properties

GCD and LCM operations are connected by strict algebraic rules that govern structural distribution, scale invariance, and multiplication identities.

### The Multiplicative Product Identity

For any two positive integers $a$ and $b$, the product of their greatest common divisor and their least common multiple is exactly equal to the product of the two integers themselves:

$$\gcd(a, b) \cdot \text{lcm}(a, b) = a \cdot b$$

This identity means that if you know the GCD of two numbers, you can calculate their LCM instantly without factoring, using the formula:
$$\text{lcm}(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\gcd(a, b)}$$

### Bézout's Identity

For any two integers $a$ and $b$ with $d = \gcd(a, b)$, there exist integers $x$ and $y$ (called **Bézout's coefficients**) such that:

$$ax + by = d$$

Furthermore, the greatest common divisor $d$ is the smallest positive integer that can be written as a linear combination of $a$ and $b$.

### Core Structural and Algebraic Rules

For all integers $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$, the following foundational properties apply:

- **Scale Invariance (Homogeneity):** Multiplying the inputs by a constant scales the resulting GCD or LCM by that same factor:
  $$\gcd(ca, cb) = c \cdot \gcd(a, b)$$
  $$\text{lcm}(ca, cb) = c \cdot \text{lcm}(a, b)$$
- **Commutativity:** The order of the inputs does not alter the output value:
  $$\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a), \quad \text{lcm}(a, b) = \text{lcm}(b, a)$$
- **Associativity:** When finding the GCD or LCM of three or more numbers, you can group the calculations in any order:
  $$\gcd(a, \gcd(b, c)) = \gcd(\gcd(a, b), c)$$
  $$\text{lcm}(a, \text{lcm}(b, c)) = \text{lcm}(\text{lcm}(a, b), c)$$
- **Distributivity:** The operations distribute over one another:
  $$\gcd(a, \text{lcm}(b, c)) = \text{lcm}(\gcd(a, b), \gcd(a, c))$$
  $$\text{lcm}(a, \gcd(b, c)) = \gcd(\text{lcm}(a, b), \text{lcm}(a, c))$$

### Comparison Matrix of GCD and LCM Configurations

The following table summarizes the structural identities and operational contrasts between GCD and LCM operations.

| Mathematical Operation                   | Prime Factorization Method                                   | Output Boundary Constraints       | Coprime Condition ($\gcd(a,b)=1$)           | Algebraic Role                                            |
| :--------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Greatest Common Divisor ($\gcd$)**     | Uses the **minimum** exponent of each common prime factor    | $1 \le \gcd(a, b) \le \min(a, b)$ | Evaluates to exactly 1                      | Identifies shared factors and controls fraction reduction |
| **Least Common Multiple ($\text{lcm}$)** | Uses the **maximum** exponent of all appearing prime factors | $\text{lcm}(a, b) \ge \max(a, b)$ | Evaluates to the direct product $a \cdot b$ | Identifies common intervals and synchronizes periods      |

## 5. Types & Variations

GCD and LCM definitions extend beyond standard integers into multi-variable sets, algebraic polynomial rings, and fractional ratios.

### 1. Multi-Element Extensions ($\ge 3$ Numbers)

The GCD and LCM of a set of three or more numbers can be found by applying the associative property to calculate the values iteratively:

$$\gcd(a, b, c) = \gcd(\gcd(a, b), c)$$

$$\text{lcm}(a, b, c) = \text{lcm}(\text{lcm}(a, b), c)$$

### 2. Fractional Fractions and Rational Ratios

GCD and LCM calculations can be extended to rational fractions. For any fractions $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$ in lowest terms:

$$\gcd\left(\frac{a}{b}, \frac{c}{d}\right) = \frac{\gcd(a, c)}{\text{lcm}(b, d)}$$

$$\text{lcm}\left(\frac{a}{b}, \frac{c}{d}\right) = \frac{\text{lcm}(a, c)}{\gcd(b, d)}$$

### 3. Polynomial Rings ($F[x]$)

In polynomial algebra, the GCD of two polynomials $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ is the polynomial of highest degree that divides both expressions without leaving a remainder. Like integer operations, polynomial GCDs can be calculated using a polynomial version of the Euclidean Algorithm, and are defined up to a scalar multiple constant.

## 6. How to Solve

Here we explore step-by-step methodologies for calculating GCD via the Euclidean Algorithm, deriving LCM using the prime factorization method, and computing Bézout's coefficients via back-substitution.

### Strategy A: Calculating the $\gcd(a, b)$ via the Euclidean Algorithm

The **Euclidean Algorithm** is an efficient method that calculates the greatest common divisor of two integers by repeatedly computing remainders until the remainder reaches zero.

**Example Question:** Calculate the Greatest Common Divisor ($\gcd$) of the integers $a = 544$ and $b = 119$.

1. **Apply the Division Algorithm to the two numbers:**
   Divide the larger number by the smaller number and record the quotient and remainder:
   $$544 = 119 \cdot 4 + 68$$
   The remainder is $68$, which is non-zero ($r \neq 0$).

2. **Shift terms and repeat the division process:**
   Move the previous divisor to the dividend position, and the previous remainder to the divisor position:
   $$119 = 68 \cdot 1 + 51$$
   The new remainder is $51$, which is still non-zero.

3. **Repeat the process again:**
   $$68 = 51 \cdot 1 + 17$$
   The new remainder is $17$.

4. **Repeat the process once more:**
   $$51 = 17 \cdot 3 + 0$$
   The remainder has evaluated to exactly $0$.

5. **Identify the final answer:**
   According to the Euclidean Algorithm, the greatest common divisor is the last non-zero remainder in the sequence.

Therefore, $\gcd(544, 119) = 17$.

### Strategy B: Calculating LCM via Canonical Prime Factorization

To calculate both the GCD and LCM of a set of numbers using prime factorization, you break the numbers down into their prime components and evaluate their minimum or maximum exponents.

**Example Question:** Find the $\gcd(a, b)$ and $\text{lcm}(a, b)$ for the integers $a = 72$ and $b = 90$.

1. **Find the unique prime factorization of each number:**
   $$72 = 8 \cdot 9 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2$$
   $$90 = 10 \cdot 9 = 2^1 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^1$$

2. **Align the prime factors using explicit zero exponents for clarity:**
   $$72 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^0$$
   $$90 = 2^1 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^1$$

3. **Calculate the GCD using the minimum exponent of each prime factor:**
   $$\gcd(72, 90) = 2^{\min(3,1)} \cdot 3^{\min(2,2)} \cdot 5^{\min(0,1)}$$
   $$\gcd(72, 90) = 2^1 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^0 = 2 \cdot 9 \cdot 1 = 18$$

4. **Calculate the LCM using the maximum exponent of each prime factor:**
   $$\text{lcm}(72, 90) = 2^{\max(3,1)} \cdot 3^{\max(2,2)} \cdot 5^{\max(0,1)}$$
   $$\text{lcm}(72, 90) = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^1 = 8 \cdot 9 \cdot 5 = 360$$

5. **Verify the product identity relation ($\gcd \cdot \text{lcm} = a \cdot b$):**
   $$\gcd(72, 90) \cdot \text{lcm}(72, 90) = 18 \cdot 360 = 6480$$
   $$a \cdot b = 72 \cdot 90 = 6480$$
   The identity holds true, confirming the calculations are correct.

### Strategy C: Finding Bézout's Coefficients via Extended Reverse Substitution

By running the Euclidean Algorithm in reverse, you can express the $\gcd(a,b)$ as a linear combination of the original numbers ($ax + by = \gcd(a,b)$).

**Example Question:** Find the integer coefficients $x$ and $y$ that satisfy Bézout's Identity for $544x + 119y = \gcd(544, 119)$.

1. **Review the division steps recorded from Strategy A:**
   - Equation 1: $544 = 119 \cdot 4 + 68 \implies 68 = 544 - 119 \cdot 4$
   - Equation 2: $119 = 68 \cdot 1 + 51 \implies 51 = 119 - 68 \cdot 1$
   - Equation 3: $68 = 51 \cdot 1 + 17 \implies 17 = 68 - 51 \cdot 1$

2. **Start with the final equation that isolates the GCD value (17):**
   $$17 = 68 - 51 \cdot 1$$

3. **Substitute the expression for the remainder 51 from Equation 2 into this equation:**
   $$17 = 68 - (119 - 68 \cdot 1) \cdot 1$$
   Distribute the terms and group like coefficients together:
   $$17 = 68 \cdot 1 - 119 \cdot 1 + 68 \cdot 1$$
   $$17 = 68 \cdot 2 - 119 \cdot 1$$

4. **Substitute the expression for the remainder 68 from Equation 1 into this combined equation:**
   $$17 = (544 - 119 \cdot 4) \cdot 2 - 119 \cdot 1$$
   Distribute the scalar factor across the terms:
   $$17 = 544 \cdot 2 - 119 \cdot 8 - 119 \cdot 1$$
   Group the final coefficients together:
   $$17 = 544 \cdot (2) + 119 \cdot (-9)$$

5. **Identify the coefficients:**
   The coefficients matching the form $544x + 119y = 17$ are:
   $$x = 2, \quad y = -9$$

Checking the arithmetic confirms that $544(2) + 119(-9) = 1088 - 1071 = 17$.

## 7. Summary

- **Dual Multiplicative Operators:** The Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) finds the largest positive integer that divides two numbers evenly, while the Least Common Multiple (LCM) finds the smallest positive integer that is a multiple of both numbers.
- **The Balancing Product Identity:** For any two positive integers $a$ and $b$, their common factors and multiples are linked by the identity formula: $\gcd(a, b) \cdot \text{lcm}(a, b) = a \cdot b$.
- **Algorithmic Efficiency:** The Euclidean Algorithm calculates the GCD of two numbers through a fast, iterative division process, avoiding the need to compute complete prime factor trees.
- **Diophantine Solvability:** Bézout's Identity shows that the GCD of two numbers can always be written as a linear combination of those numbers ($ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$). This identity determines whether an integer solution path exists for linear Diophantine equations.
- **Universal Extensions:** GCD and LCM operations expand beyond standard integers, providing a framework for analyzing rational fractions, multi-variable sets, and algebraic polynomial rings.
