# Algebra: Linear Transformations

---

## 1. Definition

In linear algebra, a **linear transformation** (also referred to as a linear map, linear mapping, or vector space homomorphism) is a specific type of mathematical function mapping between two vector spaces that preserves the underlying algebraic structure of vector addition and scalar multiplication.

Formally, let $V$ and $W$ be two vector spaces over the same underlying scalar field $F$ (such as the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ or complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$). A function or mapping $T: V \to W$ is classified as a **linear transformation** if and only if it simultaneously satisfies the following two foundational axioms for all vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$ and every scalar $c \in F$:

### 1. Additivity (Preservation of Vector Addition)

The transformation of the sum of two vectors must equal the sum of the individual transformations of those vectors:
$$T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$$

### 2. Homogeneity (Preservation of Scalar Multiplication)

The transformation of a scaled vector must equal the scalar multiplier times the transformation of the original vector:
$$T(c\mathbf{v}) = cT(\mathbf{v})$$

These two criteria are frequently consolidated into a single unified condition known as the **Principle of Superposition**. A mapping $T: V \to W$ is linear if and only if:
$$T(c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v}) = cT(\mathbf{u}) + dT(\mathbf{v})$$
for all vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$ and all scalars $c, d \in F$.

---

## 2. Key Terminology

To analyze the structural properties, structural shifts, and dimensional boundaries of linear transformations, the following precise vocabulary must be established:

- **Domain ($V$):** The originating vector space containing the set of input vectors upon which the linear transformation $T$ operates.
- **Codomain ($W$):** The target vector space that contains the set of all potential output vectors generated by the transformation.
- **Kernel / Null Space ($\ker(T)$ or $\text{N}(T)$):** The specialized subspace of the domain $V$ consisting of all vectors that are mapped directly to the zero vector $\mathbf{0}_W$ in the codomain:
  $$\ker(T) = \{ \mathbf{v} \in V \mid T(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}_W \}$$
- **Image / Range ($\text{Im}(T)$ or $\text{R}(T)$):** The specialized subspace of the codomain $W$ consisting of all vectors that are actually achieved as outputs from translating inputs from $V$:
  $$\text{Im}(T) = \{ \mathbf{w} \in W \mid \exists \mathbf{v} \in V \text{ such that } T(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{w} \}$$
- **Nullity:** The exact mathematical dimension of the kernel subspace, denoted as $\dim(\ker(T))$. It measures the degree of structural compression or "loss of information" caused by the mapping.
- **Rank:** The exact mathematical dimension of the image subspace, denoted as $\dim(\text{Im}(T))$. It measures the dimensional span of the output space.
- **Standard Matrix ($[T]$):** The unique matrix that represents a linear transformation mapping from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ relative to the standard bases. It satisfies the algebraic identity $T(\mathbf{v}) = [T]\mathbf{v}$.
- **Linear Operator:** A specialized linear transformation where the domain and the codomain are the exact same vector space ($T: V \to V$).

## 3. Purpose

Linear transformations serve as the dynamic engine of linear algebra, translating static vector spaces into functional frameworks capable of representing physical, geometric, and computational changes.

### Mapping Structural Invariance

In abstract algebra, mathematicians study functions that preserve the internal arithmetic operations of the objects they map. Linear transformations allow us to analyze how vector spaces relate to one another. By studying how a linear transformation behaves on a simple set of basis vectors, we can instantly deduce its behavior across an entire infinite vector space.

### Linearization of Complex Systems

Most natural phenomena in physics, economics, and fluid dynamics are inherently non-linear and incredibly difficult to solve directly. Linear transformations provide the foundation for **linearization**—the process of approximating complex non-linear systems locally using linear systems (via Jacobian matrices in multi-variable calculus). This makes highly complex tracking models solvable.

### Real-World Engineering Applications

- **Computer Graphics and CGI:** Every visual shift in a three-dimensional computer game—whether it involves rotating a character, scaling a texture, translating coordinates, or projecting a 3D scene onto a flat 2D screen monitor—is executed computationally as a matrix-driven linear transformation.
- **Data Science & Cryptography:** In machine learning, high-dimensional consumer datasets are projected onto smaller, optimized coordinate maps via linear transformations like Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). In network security, multi-stage encryption ciphers apply linear operations to scramble data arrays without destroying structural decipherability.
- **Differential Equations:** In advanced physics and calculus, the operations of taking a derivative ($\frac{d}{dx}$) or evaluating an integral form valid linear transformations. This allows complex differential networks to be analyzed using standard linear algebra vector techniques.

## 4. Fundamental Properties

Linear transformations possess highly consistent structural laws that govern zero mappings, algebraic combinations, and dimensional preservation.

### Core Algebraic Properties

For any valid linear transformation $T: V \to W$:

- **Preservation of the Zero Vector:** A linear transformation must always map the zero vector of the domain to the zero vector of the codomain:
  $$T(\mathbf{0}_V) = \mathbf{0}_W$$
- **Preservation of Additive Inverses:** The transformation of a negated vector is equal to the negation of the transformed vector:
  $$T(-\mathbf{v}) = -T(\mathbf{v})$$
- **Linear Combination Expansion:** The transformation maps across arbitrary finite linear combinations according to the formula:
  $$T(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k) = c_1T(\mathbf{v}_1) + c_2T(\mathbf{v}_2) + \dots + c_kT(\mathbf{v}_k)$$

### The Rank-Nullity Theorem

One of the most vital theorems in linear algebra connects the dimensions of the kernel and the image of a linear transformation to the overall dimension of its originating domain. If $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space and $T: V \to W$ is a linear transformation, then:

$$\dim(\ker(T)) + \dim(\text{Im}(T)) = \dim(V)$$

$$\text{Nullity} + \text{Rank} = \text{Dimension of Domain}$$

### Matrix of Linear Transformation Classifications

The following table summarizes the structural conditions that classify linear transformations based on their mapping properties.

| Classification | Alternative Name | Kernel / Null Space Condition                       | Image / Range Condition                 | Dimensional Constraint |
| :------------- | :--------------- | :-------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------- | :--------------------- |
| **Injective**  | One-to-One       | $\ker(T) = \{\mathbf{0}_V\}$ (Nullity = 0)          | Distinct inputs yield distinct outputs  | $\dim(V) \le \dim(W)$  |
| **Surjective** | Onto             | No constraint on kernel contents                    | $\text{Im}(T) = W$ (Rank = $\dim(W)$)   | $\dim(V) \ge \dim(W)$  |
| **Bijective**  | Isomorphism      | $\ker(T) = \{\mathbf{0}_V\}$ and $\text{Im}(T) = W$ | Maps space into an identical twin space | $\dim(V) = \dim(W)$    |

## 5. Types & Variations

Linear transformations are expressed through different formats depending on whether they act as geometric manipulations, matrix calculations, or calculus operations.

### 1. Geometric Linear Operators in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$

These transformations structurally alter geometric coordinate space while keeping the origin $(0,0)$ completely fixed in place.

- **Rotation:** Rotates vectors counterclockwise through an angle $\theta$ in a two-dimensional plane via the standard matrix operator:
  $$[T] = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$$
- **Reflection:** Flips a vector across a target line or plane axis. For example, reflecting a vector across the horizontal $x$-axis utilizes the operator:
  $$[T] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$
- **Scaling / Dilation:** Stretches or compresses space by scaling coordinates uniformly by a factor $k$:
  $$[T] = \begin{pmatrix} k & 0 \\ 0 & k \end{pmatrix}$$

### 2. Matrix Transformations

Every fixed $m \times n$ matrix $A$ over a field $F$ automatically defines a valid linear transformation $T_A: F^n \to F^m$ through matrix-vector multiplication:
$$T_A(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}$$
Because matrix multiplication distributes over matrix addition and commutes with scalar multiplication, all matrix transformations are fundamentally linear transformations.

### 3. Calculus-Based Linear Operators

Linear transformations extend naturally to infinite-dimensional function spaces.

- **The Derivative Operator:** Let $D: P_n \to P_{n-1}$ be the derivative mapping defined by $D(p(x)) = p'(x)$. Because the derivative of a sum equals the sum of the derivatives, and constants can be factored out, $D$ is a linear transformation.
- **The Definite Integral Operator:** The evaluation of an integral across a closed interval forms a linear transformation mapping a continuous function space to a scalar real value field.

## 6. How to Solve

Here we explore step-by-step methodologies for verifying map linearity, deriving standard matrices from basis vectors, and applying the Rank-Nullity Theorem to solve abstract dimensional problems.

### Strategy A: Verifying the Linearity of a Mathematical Transformation

To prove whether a given functional mapping is a valid linear transformation, you must test whether it satisfies both additivity and homogeneity under abstract vector distribution.

**Example Question:** Prove whether the mapping $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $T\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x + 2y \\ 3x \end{pmatrix}$ is a valid linear transformation.

1. **Test for Vector Additivity:**
   Select two arbitrary independent vectors $\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \end{pmatrix}$.
   - **Step 1: Compute the transformation of the combined sum ($T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v})$):**
     $$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + x_2 \\ y_1 + y_2 \end{pmatrix}$$
     $$T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} (x_1 + x_2) + 2(y_1 + y_2) \\ 3(x_1 + x_2) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + x_2 + 2y_1 + 2y_2 \\ 3x_1 + 3x_2 \end{pmatrix}$$
   - **Step 2: Compute the sum of the individual transformations ($T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$):**
     $$T(\mathbf{u}) = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + 2y_1 \\ 3x_1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad T(\mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} x_2 + 2y_2 \\ 3x_2 \end{pmatrix}$$
     $$T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v}) = \begin{pmatrix} (x_1 + 2y_1) + (x_2 + 2y_2) \\ 3x_1 + 3x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + x_2 + 2y_1 + 2y_2 \\ 3x_1 + 3x_2 \end{pmatrix}$$
     Comparing the results shows that $T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$. Additivity is satisfied.

2. **Test for Scalar Homogeneity:**
   Multiply vector $\mathbf{u}$ by an arbitrary scalar constant $c$:
   - **Step 1: Compute the transformation of the scaled vector ($T(c\mathbf{u})$):**
     $$c\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} cx_1 \\ cy_1 \end{pmatrix} \implies T(c\mathbf{u}) = \begin{pmatrix} (cx_1) + 2(cy_1) \\ 3(cx_1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} cx_1 + 2cy_1 \\ 3cx_1 \end{pmatrix}$$
   - **Step 2: Compute the scalar multiplication applied to the transformed output ($cT(\mathbf{u})$):**
     $$cT(\mathbf{u}) = c \begin{pmatrix} x_1 + 2y_1 \\ 3x_1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c(x_1 + 2y_1) \\ c(3x_1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} cx_1 + 2cy_1 \\ 3cx_1 \end{pmatrix}$$
     Comparing the results shows that $T(c\mathbf{u}) = cT(\mathbf{u})$. Homogeneity is satisfied.

Because both core operational axioms are verified, the mapping $T$ is a **valid linear transformation**.

### Strategy B: Constructing the Standard Matrix of a Linear Transformation

If you know how a linear transformation maps the standard basis vectors of a space, you can construct its standard matrix operator directly.

**Example Question:** Find the standard matrix $[T]$ for a linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ that maps the standard basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ as follows:

$$T(\mathbf{e}_1) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}, \quad T(\mathbf{e}_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$$

1. **Recall the column construction rule for standard matrices:**
   The standard matrix $[T]$ of a linear transformation is formed by setting the transformations of the standard basis vectors as the consecutive columns of the matrix:
   $$[T] = \begin{pmatrix} \mid & \mid \\ T(\mathbf{e}_1) & T(\mathbf{e}_2) \\ \mid & \mid \end{pmatrix}$$

2. **Arrange the output vectors as columns:**
   $$[T] = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 \\ 5 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$$

3. **Verify the matrix operator functionality:**
   Test the matrix by multiplying it by an arbitrary coordinate vector $\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$:
   $$T(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 \\ 5 & -3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2x + 0y \\ -1x + 4y \\ 5x - 3y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2x \\ -x + 4y \\ 5x - 3y \end{pmatrix}$$

The derived matrix accurately maps the linear transformation.

### Strategy C: Finding the Kernel Subspace Matrix Coordinates

Locating the kernel means finding all vector solutions that map to the zero vector. This is equivalent to finding the null space of the transformation's standard matrix.

**Example Question:** Find a basis for the kernel of the linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ represented by the matrix:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 4 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$$

1. **Set up the homogeneous matrix equation:**
   To find the kernel, we must find all vectors $\mathbf{x}$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$:
   $$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 4 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

2. **Perform Gaussian Elimination to find the row-reduced echelon form (RREF):**
   Subtract twice the first row from the second row ($\text{Row 2} \to \text{Row 2} - 2\cdot\text{Row 1}$):
   $$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

3. **Analyze the free variable constraints:**
   The single remaining non-zero equation is $x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 0$.
   - $x_1$ is a pivot variable.
   - $x_2$ and $x_3$ are free variables. Let $x_2 = s$ and $x_3 = t$, where $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$.

4. **Express the pivot variable in terms of the free parameters:**
   $$x_1 = -2x_2 + x_3 \implies x_1 = -2s + t$$

5. **Write the parametric solution as a vector combination:**
   $$\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2s + t \\ s \\ t \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2s \\ s \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} t \\ 0 \\ t \end{pmatrix} = s \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

6. **Isolate the distinct basis vectors:**
   The vectors spanning the null space form the basis for the kernel:
   $$\text{Basis for } \ker(T) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$$

The nullity of this transformation is exactly 2. By the Rank-Nullity Theorem, its rank must be $3 - 2 = 1$.

## 7. Summary

- **Structure-Preserving Maps:** A linear transformation is a specialized function mapping between vector spaces that preserves the core algebraic operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication.
- **The Core Subspaces:** Every linear transformation is characterized by two fundamental subspaces: the **Kernel** (the set of inputs that map to zero, tracking compression) and the **Image** (the set of all achieved outputs, tracking dimension expansion).
- **Dimensional Balancing:** The Rank-Nullity Theorem establishes that the dimension of the kernel plus the dimension of the image always balances to equal the total dimension of the domain space.
- **Matrix Equivalences:** For finite-dimensional vector spaces, every linear transformation can be represented as a unique matrix operator. This allows abstract functional maps to be evaluated using standard matrix arithmetic.
- **Geometric Action:** Linear operations provide the foundational math for spatial transformations like rotations, reflections, and scaling, which keeps the coordinate origin fixed in place.
