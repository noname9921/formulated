# Algebra: Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

---

## 1. Definition

In linear algebra, **eigenvalues** and **eigenvectors** reveal the internal structural characteristics of a linear transformation. When a transformation is applied to a space, most vectors change both their magnitude and their directional orientation. However, there exist unique, non-zero vectors that maintain their exact directional alignment, experiencing only a scaling effect.

Formally, let $A$ be an $n \times n$ square matrix over a scalar field $F$ (typically the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ or complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$). A non-zero vector $\mathbf{v} \in F^n$ is classified as an **eigenvector** of $A$ if there exists a scalar $\lambda \in F$ such that the following fundamental equation is satisfied:

$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$$

Where:

- $A$ is the $n \times n$ structural matrix operator.
- $\mathbf{v}$ is the **eigenvector**, which must strictly be non-zero ($\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$).
- $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue** corresponding to the eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$. It represents the scaling factor along that directional axis.

This relationship states that multiplying the matrix $A$ by the vector $\mathbf{v}$ yields an output vector that is a direct scalar multiple of $\mathbf{v}$. The complex matrix operation is simplified into standard scalar multiplication along these specific directions.

---

## 2. Key Terminology

To analyze spectral decomposition and diagonal transformations effectively, the following precise vocabulary must be established:

- **Characteristic Equation:** The polynomial equation derived by setting the determinant of the shifted matrix to zero, used to find the eigenvalues of $A$:
  $$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$
- **Characteristic Polynomial ($p(\lambda)$):** The polynomial of degree $n$ generated by evaluating the expansion of $\det(A - \lambda I)$.
- **Eigenspace ($E_\lambda$):** The set of all eigenvectors corresponding to a specific eigenvalue $\lambda$, combined with the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$. It forms a valid subspace of $F^n$ and is defined as the null space of the shifted matrix:
  $$E_\lambda = \ker(A - \lambda I)$$
- **Algebraic Multiplicity ($m_\lambda$):** The number of times a specific eigenvalue appears as a root of the characteristic polynomial equation.
- **Geometric Multiplicity ($g_\lambda$):** The dimension of the eigenspace $E_\lambda$ corresponding to $\lambda$. It represents the maximum number of linearly independent eigenvectors associated with that eigenvalue.
- **Diagonalization:** The algebraic process of transforming a square matrix $A$ into a diagonal matrix $D$ through a similarity transformation using a matrix of eigenvectors $P$:
  $$D = P^{-1}AP$$
- **Spectrum ($\sigma(A)$):** The complete set of all eigenvalues associated with a given square matrix $A$.

---

## 3. Purpose

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors act as a mathematical diagnostic tool. They break down complex multi-dimensional operations into independent, one-dimensional scaling steps.

### Simplifying Matrix Computations

Evaluating large powers of a standard square matrix (such as calculating $A^{100}$) by hand or through raw code is computationally expensive and prone to rounding errors. If a matrix can be broken down into its constituent eigenvalues and eigenvectors through diagonalization, computing high powers scales linearly:

$$A^k = P D^k P^{-1}$$

Because raising a diagonal matrix $D$ to a power requires only raising its diagonal elements to that power, this transformation simplifies high-order polynomial calculations.

### Uncoupling Differential Networks

In advanced physics, engineering, and population ecology, systems are often governed by networks of coupled differential equations where the rate of change of one variable depends on the states of all other variables. Rewriting these systems using an eigenbasis uncouples the equations into independent, isolated, one-dimensional differential equations that can be solved directly.

### Real-World Engineering Applications

- **PageRank Search Algorithms:** Google's foundational web search engine models internet browsing as a massive transition matrix. The rank or importance of web pages is determined by computing the dominant eigenvector corresponding to the maximum eigenvalue ($\lambda = 1$) of a probability matrix.
- **Principal Component Analysis (PCA):** In data science and machine learning, massive datasets with hundreds of variables are simplified by calculating the eigenvectors of the data's covariance matrix. The eigenvectors pointing along the largest eigenvalues represent the axes of maximum statistical variance.
- **Structural Resonance Analysis:** Mechanical and aerospace engineers compute the eigenvalues of structural stiffness matrices to identify the natural resonant frequencies of bridges, buildings, and aircraft. If external vibrations match these frequencies, structural failure can occur.

## 4. Fundamental Properties

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors exhibit consistent algebraic properties under summation, transposition, and similarity mappings.

### Multiplicity and Diagonalization Boundaries

For any valid eigenvalue $\lambda$:

- **Multiplicity Constraint:** The geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue can never exceed its algebraic multiplicity:
  $$1 \le g_\lambda \le m_\lambda$$
- **Diagonalization Criterion:** An $n \times n$ square matrix $A$ can be completely diagonalized if and only if the sum of the geometric multiplicities of its eigenvalues equals $n$. This requires $g_\lambda = m_\lambda$ for every individual eigenvalue in its spectrum.

### Linear Independence of Spectral Axes

If $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_k$ are distinct eigenvalues of a matrix $A$, and $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k$ are their corresponding eigenvectors, then the set of vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ is guaranteed to be **linearly independent**.

### Matrix Trace and Determinant Connections

Two important scalar values of a matrix—the **Trace** ($\text{tr}(A)$, the sum of the entries on the main diagonal) and the **Determinant** ($\det(A)$)—are directly related to its eigenvalues. For an $n \times n$ matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$ (counted with their algebraic multiplicities):

$$\text{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ii} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_n$$

$$\det(A) = \lambda_1 \cdot \lambda_2 \dots \lambda_n$$

### Matrix of Invariant Operational Shifts

The following table summarizes how performing standard operations on a matrix shifts its corresponding eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

| Modified Matrix               | Resulting Eigenvalues                             | Resulting Eigenvectors        | Mathematical Justification                                                                                                               |
| :---------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ | :---------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Matrix Power ($A^k$)**      | $\lambda^k$                                       | $\mathbf{v}$ (Unchanged)      | $A^2\mathbf{v} = A(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda(A\mathbf{v}) = \lambda^2\mathbf{v}$                                                      |
| **Scalar Scale ($cA$)**       | $c\lambda$                                        | $\mathbf{v}$ (Unchanged)      | $(cA)\mathbf{v} = c(A\mathbf{v}) = c(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = (c\lambda)\mathbf{v}$                                                          |
| **Identity Shift ($A + cI$)** | $\lambda + c$                                     | $\mathbf{v}$ (Unchanged)      | $(A + cI)\mathbf{v} = A\mathbf{v} + c\mathbf{v} = (\lambda + c)\mathbf{v}$                                                               |
| **Inverse Matrix ($A^{-1}$)** | $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ _(provided $\lambda \neq 0$)_ | $\mathbf{v}$ (Unchanged)      | $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \implies \mathbf{v} = \lambda A^{-1}\mathbf{v} \implies A^{-1}\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{v}$ |
| **Transpose Matrix ($A^T$)**  | $\lambda$                                         | Changes to new tracking paths | $\det(A^T - \lambda I) = \det((A - \lambda I)^T) = \det(A - \lambda I)$                                                                  |

## 5. Types & Variations

Matrices with specific structural symmetries or configurations yield highly predictable patterns in their eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

### 1. Symmetric Real Matrices ($A = A^T$)

When a matrix is completely symmetric and contains only real number entries, it satisfies the **Spectral Theorem**:

- All eigenvalues are guaranteed to be real numbers ($\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}$), even if the characteristic polynomial permits complex roots.
- Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are completely **orthogonal** to one another ($\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 0$).
- The matrix is always diagonalizeable, using an orthogonal matrix of eigenvectors ($P^{-1} = P^T$).

### 2. Triangular Matrices (Upper, Lower, or Diagonal)

If a square matrix is upper triangular, lower triangular, or purely diagonal, its eigenvalues do not require polynomial calculation.

> **Triangular Shortcut Rule:** The eigenvalues of any upper triangular, lower triangular, or diagonal matrix are exactly the scalar entries located along its main diagonal:
> $$\sigma(A) = \{a_{11}, a_{22}, \dots, a_{nn}\}$$

### 3. Complex Hermitians and Orthogonals

- **Hermitian Matrix ($A = A^*$):** The complex equivalent of a symmetric matrix, where the matrix equals its conjugate transpose. Like real symmetric matrices, its eigenvalues are always real numbers.
- **Orthogonal Matrix ($A^T A = I$):** Real matrices that preserve vector lengths during transformations. The eigenvalues of an orthogonal matrix always have an absolute value modulus equal to 1 ($|\lambda| = 1$), which maps to rotations or reflections.

## 6. How to Solve

Here we explore step-by-step methodologies for extracting eigenvalues, constructing corresponding eigenspaces, and setting up full diagonalization similarity matrices.

### Strategy A: Finding Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a $2 \times 2$ Matrix

To extract the spectral components of a matrix, we first compute its eigenvalues using the characteristic equation, then solve the corresponding homogeneous systems to locate their eigenvectors.

**Example Question:** Find all eigenvalues and a basis for each eigenspace of the matrix $A$:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

1. **Set up the characteristic equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$:**
   $$A - \lambda I = \begin{pmatrix} 4 - \lambda & -2 \\ 1 & 1 - \lambda \end{pmatrix}$$
   $$\det(A - \lambda I) = (4 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda) - (-2)(1) = 0$$

2. **Expand the polynomial expression to find the roots:**
   $$4 - 4\lambda - \lambda + \lambda^2 + 2 = 0$$
   $$\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 6 = 0$$
   $$(\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 3) = 0$$
   The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 2$ and $\lambda_2 = 3$. Each eigenvalue has an algebraic multiplicity of 1.

3. **Construct the Eigenspace for $\lambda_1 = 2$:**
   Substitute $\lambda = 2$ into the shifted matrix equation $(A - 2I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$:
   $$\begin{pmatrix} 4 - 2 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 - 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
   Perform row reduction. Row 1 is a multiple of Row 2, which reduces the system to:
   $$1v_1 - 1v_2 = 0 \implies v_1 = v_2$$
   Let the free variable $v_2 = t$. The parametric solution vector is:
   $$\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} t \\ t \end{pmatrix} = t \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
   The basis vector for the eigenspace $E_2$ is:
   $$\text{Basis for } E_2 = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$$

4. **Construct the Eigenspace for $\lambda_2 = 3$:**
   Substitute $\lambda = 3$ into the shifted matrix equation $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$:
   $$\begin{pmatrix} 4 - 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 - 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
   Row reduction simplifies the system to:
   $$1v_1 - 2v_2 = 0 \implies v_1 = 2v_2$$
   Let the free variable $v_2 = t$. The parametric solution vector is:
   $$\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 2t \\ t \end{pmatrix} = t \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
   The basis vector for the eigenspace $E_3$ is:
   $$\text{Basis for } E_3 = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$$

### Strategy B: Executing Matrix Diagonalization

Once a matrix's eigenvalues and eigenvectors are known, they can be assembled into a similarity transformation that simplifies the matrix into a diagonal form.

**Example Question:** Diagonalize the matrix $A$ from Strategy A, and verify the relation $D = P^{-1}AP$.

1. **Construct the modal matrix $P$ using the discovered eigenvectors as columns:**
   $$P = \begin{pmatrix} \mid & \mid \\ \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 \\ \mid & \mid \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

2. **Construct the diagonal matrix $D$ using the corresponding eigenvalues in the exact same order:**
   $$D = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$

3. **Calculate the inverse matrix $P^{-1}$:**
   $$\det(P) = (1)(1) - (2)(1) = 1 - 2 = -1$$
   $$P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-1} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

4. **Verify the similarity transformation $P^{-1}AP = D$:**
   First, compute the product $AP$:
   $$AP = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} (4-2) & (8-2) \\ (1+1) & (2+1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$
   Now, multiply by $P^{-1}$:
   $$P^{-1}(AP) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} (-2+4) & (-6+6) \\ (2-2) & (6-3) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = D$$

The diagonalization is verified.

## 7. Summary

- **Directional Invariance:** Eigenvectors are non-zero vectors whose directions remain unchanged when a linear transformation is applied. Eigenvalues are the scalar factors ($\lambda$) that scale these vectors.
- **The Spectral Kernel:** Eigenvalues are found by calculating the roots of the characteristic equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. Eigenspaces are then constructed by finding the null space of the resulting shifted matrix.
- **Algebraic vs. Geometric Multiplicity:** The algebraic multiplicity tracks how often an eigenvalue appears as a root, while the geometric multiplicity tracks the number of linearly independent eigenvectors it possesses. For a matrix to be diagonalizable, these two values must match for all eigenvalues.
- **Trace and Determinant Invariants:** The sum of a matrix's eigenvalues always equals its trace, and their product always equals its determinant.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Diagonalization ($A = PDP^{-1}$) simplifies high-order matrix calculations by converting complex matrix operations into element-wise scalar operations along the eigenaxes.
